The **Hindenburg** (LZ 129) and its sister ship, the Graf Zeppelin II, were the largest aircraft ever flown: **245 m long, 41.2 m across**, holding about **200,000 m³ of hydrogen**. It flew transatlantic passenger services from 1936, until it caught fire landing at Lakehurst, New Jersey, on **6 May 1937**: 36 people died, and Herbert Morrison's radio report ("Oh, the humanity!") ended the era of the passenger airship.

It sits on the air line for the same reason every airship does: floating means weighing about as much as the air you push aside. Loaded, it weighed roughly **220 t** (about 118 t empty), about 1.1 kg per cubic metre of envelope, against 1.2 kg/m³ for air. It is about 25 times heavier than a modern Zeppelin NT, yet just as "dense".

It was designed for helium, which the United States wouldn't export. Hydrogen lifts only about 8% more (it is 0.09 kg/m³ against helium's 0.18 and air's 1.2), so on a log chart the gas barely moves it: what hydrogen changed was that it burns.
