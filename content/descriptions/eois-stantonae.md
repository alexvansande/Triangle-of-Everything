*Eois stantonae* is a small moth from the Amazon rainforest around Manaus, Brazil, described in 2026. Its forewings are yellow, crossed by irregular rust-brown stripes; it has a rust-brown collar behind the head and a yellow abdomen with rust-brown spots along its sides. Like the rest of its genus it is tiny: *Eois* moths have wingspans of up to about 2 cm.

**A species hiding in plain sight.** For decades it was lumped together with its look-alikes under a single name, *Eois russearia*. In 2026 a team of researchers (Moraes, Yamaguchi, Kato and colleagues) took that "one species" apart using integrative taxonomy: they compared DNA, the fine anatomy of the wings and genitalia, the plants each caterpillar eats and where each population lives. The single name turned out to hide several distinct species, and they described eight new ones. *E. stantonae* is one of them; several of its siblings were named in honor of the Orixás, the deities of Afro-Brazilian religions.

**A life on Piper.** *Eois* caterpillars are specialists: each species feeds on plants of the pepper genus *Piper*, which defend themselves with a rich and varied arsenal of chemicals. The caterpillars of *E. stantonae* were found on *Piper hostmannianum*. That host plant is part of what told the new species apart from its neighbors.

**Mariana Alves Stanton.** The species is named in honor of Mariana Alves Stanton, a Brazilian chemical ecologist who died in 2024. She studied biology and ecology at the University of Campinas (Unicamp), earned her doctorate at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology in Jena, Germany, and continued her research at the University of São Paulo. Her work asked how the chemical diversity of *Piper* plants shapes the insects that feed on them — specialist herbivores like *Eois* — and the parasitoids that hunt those insects, including the scents plants release when they are attacked. The authors chose her name as a posthumous tribute: a moth that lives on exactly the plants she spent her career understanding.
