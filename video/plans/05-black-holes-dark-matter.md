# Video 5 (planned): black holes, dark matter, the largest objects

What the app's tour (`content/tour-content.md`: black-holes, dark-matter, largest)
and the old full-tour narration (take 1, 2026-10-06, 12:16–23:00; in git history
under `video/narration-take-1/raw-transcript.md`) already say, checked against the
chart (`src/objects.json`) and the physics.

## What was said, in order

1. **The Schwarzschild line is the first side.** Any object, compressed enough,
   becomes a black hole. A black hole has no real size (the singularity), but it
   has a radius of no return, proportional to mass: hence the 1 : 1 slope (45°).
   Black holes can have any mass; stars make them, so did the early universe.
2. **Primordial black holes.** Most would have evaporated (Hawking radiation);
   survivors would be "the mass of a small moon in the size of a tiny atom"
   (narration) / "a small asteroid… the size of an atom" (tour). Invisible, felt
   only by gravity: a dark-matter candidate.
3. **A second, lighter density line beyond the stars:** "the density of dark
   matter": supernova remnants, stellar nurseries, globular clusters, dwarf
   galaxies, the Milky Way, groups, clusters, Laniakea, then voids ("no stars, but
   they have mass").
4. **The Hubble radius is the second side**: "the limit at which anything can be
   seen by us… nothing can be bigger". The universe is probably infinite; this is
   the observable universe.
5. **The observable universe lands where the Schwarzschild line meets the Hubble
   radius.** "Is our universe a black hole? Probably not": "it was not always on
   the Schwarzschild line", and it's only round from where we are.

## Fact check

Right: 1 (singularity = GR's prediction; r_s = 2GM/c²); PBHs below about 5×10¹⁴ g
would have evaporated by now; the asteroid-to-small-moon mass range (about
10¹⁷–10²³ g) is still open as a dark-matter candidate; galaxies are mostly dark
matter; voids hold some mass, just less than average; we can't know what's beyond
our horizon; "probably not a black hole" is the right conclusion.

Wrong, worth saying differently:
- **"The Hubble radius is the limit of what we can see."** A common mix-up. The
  Hubble radius (about 14 billion light-years) is where space recedes from us at
  the speed of light; we see well beyond it (the observable universe is about 46
  billion light-years in radius). Lineweaver & Patel use the Hubble radius as the
  largest size anything can have and still act as one object (no signal can
  cross it within the age of the universe, roughly). Note the chart's "Observable
  Universe" dot sits at the Hubble radius (10²⁸·¹ cm ≈ 14.6 billion ly), not at
  46. Suggested: "This edge is the Hubble radius: the distance at which space
  itself carries things away from us faster than light. Nothing bigger than that
  can hold together as one thing."
- **"It was not always on the Schwarzschild line."** In the paper it was: the
  Hubble sphere of a flat universe always holds exactly the mass whose
  Schwarzschild radius equals it (that's what "critical density" means:
  r_s = 2GM/c² = c/H). Over cosmic time it slid along the black-hole line, from
  the Planck point (the Big Bang, video 1) to where it is now; the isochrons mark
  the way. So it's not a coincidence either. Why it's still not a black hole: it
  is expanding, not collapsing; it has no centre and no outside; and every
  observer has their own Hubble sphere.
- **PBH size.** The chart's "Smallest Primordial BH" (the lightest that could
  survive) is 5×10¹⁴ g (a small asteroid, a few hundred metres) in about
  10⁻¹³ cm: the size of a proton, not an atom. The dark-matter candidates are
  heavier: a moon-sized mass would be about a fiftieth of an atom across.
  Suggested: "the mass of an asteroid, squeezed smaller than an atom".
- **"Every object beyond the stars follows the dark-matter density line."** The
  purple line (10⁻²⁴ g/cm³, about one hydrogen atom per cm³) runs through the
  galaxies (Milky Way 10⁻²³·⁸, dwarf galaxies 10⁻²³). Nebulae and globular
  clusters have little or no dark matter, and the bigger structures fall well
  below it: Laniakea and the voids sit at about 10⁻³⁰, the average density of
  the universe. A better story: the larger the structure, the closer it gets to
  the average density of the universe, and at that density, the size of the
  Hubble radius lands exactly on the black-hole line (the previous point).
