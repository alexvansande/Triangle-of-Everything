title: "Triangle of Everything (Space)"
---

What is the real difference between a planet and a star? How small can a black hole be? What is the lifecycle of a star? Where is all the dark matter? Can it be all simply black holes? What's the edge of our universe?

For all these answers there's one surprisingly useful visualization tool that will tell their stories and more. It's an image that contains everything that ever was or ever can be, all plotted in a log scale of mass and size. That chart will be bounded by three limits: top left by relativity, bottom left by quantum mechanics and on the right by the size of the universe. I call it the "Triangle of Everything" and on the last video I explored the diagonal of things made of atoms, from humans to planets.

Everything is a delicate dance of a few fundamental forces of our universe. Gravity wants to push matter together, electromagnetism will make them push further apart, as electrons repel each other, while attracted to the nucleus. This diagonal line is where they reach equilibrium and it's no coincidence it's the density of atomic matter and of most things that surround us.

We tend to think of stars and planets as completely different things, but that's not really true. Jupiter for example, makes its own heat, more than it receives from the sun, all generated in its high pressure core.  And as  man planet's grows, so does that pressure, until that delicate dance steps again and now gravity wins over the next defense: the nucleus. 

These are the Brown dwarfs, too big to be planets, too small to be stars. Just big enough to fuse in its core a rare kind of hydrogen called deuterium–but not ordinary hydrogen. But deuterium is so rare, brown dwarfs just can't keep shining on like stars.

But add a little bit more mass and you get the split you see on this chart. Gravity wins over the electric repulsion that keeps protons apart and they fuse into helium and other light elements. A star is born. But that fusion releases energy which creates a new outwards push, which makes stars grow in size. Stars spend most of their lives somewhere in this diagonal line, called the Main sequence. 

There's a limit to that main sequence: light itself has an outwards pressure and if a star is too massive, it's outer layers are pushed so hard against it that they'll escape the star's own gravity. So no known star can be beyond X solar masses.

That new truce between the two forces - one pushing it to the left and the other to the right, will last a few billion years. 

The draw is broken when the fuel runs out. Once there's no more hydrogen – or sometimes helium – to burn, then a new dance begins where the star starts to rapidly collapse. But now that collapse creates even more heavier elements, which will create a new outer pressure and it will expand again, and then collapse, until all that is left is a core made of mostly iron and other heavy metals. These elements don't produce energy when they undergo fusion, quite the contrary, they steal it, so the star enters in a catastrophic core collapse which will now separate it in two distinct objects in our chart.

The outer layers will keep expanding, with the leftover energy from the last battle, and become supernova remnants, or nebulas. These will eventually meet others and merge into a larger cloud of dust called a stellar nursery, which eventually will collapse back into a porto-star and start a new cycle. But this time, the next generation will start with some richer materials that could lead to a planet full of life like ours.

The core, however stays behind. If the star isn't massive enough to go supernova, it will remain as a white dwarf, a core of carbon and oxygen. If it does go supernova, then its core collapse will be so powerful that its gravity will win once again, protons and electrons will be squeezed into a mass of pure neutrons – a neutron star. They will sit at the exact density line of the neutron. 

And if it's any bigger, then something even weirder will happen – it will collapse into a black hole.

**Black holes** (as cut, rec48, with the plant and the re-recorded search line)

Most objects on this chart are a bunch of little dots, but black holes are not. They all sit exactly on one line, called the Schwarzschild radius. And it's a very important line, because it's the top left edge of our triangle. Any object that becomes too small for its mass collapses into a black hole, and every black hole lives exactly on this line, meaning that there's no object that could ever exist above or to the left of it. **\[new, the plant →\]** Remember this line. It comes back at the very largest scale.

A black hole doesn't really have a size. It's thought to be a singularity, a point. But it does have an event horizon, the distance from which nothing can escape, and that grows in step with its mass. That's why the edge is a straight 45-degree line. Squeeze the Sun into a black hole and it would be about six kilometres wide, and the Earth would be the size of a marble.

The black holes we know formed in the collapsing cores of dying stars, or grew by swallowing gas and merging with other black holes. They go from a few Suns to billions of stars. The one in the centre of the Milky Way, for example, has four million solar masses. And here's the strange part: the bigger they get, the less dense they are. The really supermassive ones are less dense than water. They would float, if you had a pool big enough. Like M87, the first black hole ever photographed, or TON 618, one of the largest we know of. Its event horizon is bigger than Pluto's orbit.

But the most interesting question isn't how big they can be, but how small. With enough pressure, anything can become a black hole. We can't create one in the lab, but we know two places where that condition exists. The first one is, as I said, the core of a dying star. The second was the very first instants after the Big Bang, when everything was a super hot and super dense soup. Some of that soup could have collapsed into black holes of every size, and some of these might still be around.

And we've been looking for them: with quasars, with the cosmic microwave background, with gravitational waves, and finally with microlensing. And every search has come up empty, and the window where they could hide keeps closing. They can't be too small either, or they'd have evaporated by now, through a process we call Hawking radiation. What's left is a narrow window: something the size of a virus with the mass of a small moon, or a whole asteroid's mass squeezed into something smaller than an atom. And if they're out there, they'd be invisible. No light, nothing a telescope could see. We would only notice their gravity.

But that's the thing: we do see that gravity. When we weigh galaxies, they're much heavier than everything we can see in them. Five, sometimes ten times heavier. So most of the matter in the universe is something invisible, which of course we call dark matter. We don't know what it is. But if it's just tiny black holes, that's interesting, because it wouldn't need any new particle to explain it. Or, of course, maybe it's something else entirely. Maybe it isn't a thing at all, and we just got gravity wrong. But if it's something real, it's somewhere on this chart, and people have been looking for it. And not only on the black hole line. Oh no. Someone worked out that if dark matter came in lumps about this size, they'd leave tracks in the crystals of ancient rocks. So they looked at the rocks: nothing. Another team realised it can't be here either, otherwise people's heads would just randomly explode, as if shot by a cosmic bullet. So we crossed out this area. The search for dark matter is really a lot of scientists scratching off areas of this map, one by one.

But I still find it fascinating that just maybe, in the very first moments of our history, most of the matter that existed collapsed into black holes. And we, everything else, the galaxies, the stars, everything we know, are just the lucky few survivors.

**Bigger means emptier** (rec49, with the dark-matter callback and the new player) **new where marked**

Every diagonal line on this chart is a density. We saw that neutron stars have the density of a neutron, and the supermassive black holes were already less dense than water. The further right we go, the emptier things get, because the bigger something is, the less dense it is. A galaxy is mostly the empty space between its stars. Here's our own, the Milky Way, and its sister, Andromeda. We live in a small group of galaxies called the Local Group. **\[new →\]** And these are the galaxies we weighed a minute ago: five to ten times heavier than everything we can see in them. Whatever dark matter is, this is where it's doing the holding.

Galaxy clusters are even less dense, because now it's the galaxies plus all the empty space between them. But gravity still holds them together. And above that are the largest structures we know of: superclusters, which come in the shape of filaments. We live in one called Laniakea, made of about a hundred thousand galaxies.

**\[new, the new player →\]** And here, for the first time, something changes. Until now gravity has won every match: it rounded the moons, it crushed the atoms, it crushed the electrons, it made the black holes. Four to nothing. But a supercluster isn't held together. The space inside it is stretching faster than gravity can pull it back, and in the far future Laniakea will have come apart. So at the very largest scale there's a new player, and it isn't one of the four forces. We call it dark energy. **\[score: a third column, Dark energy 1\]**

There's still something larger: voids. Voids are just empty regions of space. Not completely empty, just emptier than the space around them. So they do have mass, and they fit on the chart.

**The second edge** (rec49 + rec50, lightly fixed)

> But can anything be bigger than that? Try to go any bigger, and you reach the second edge: the Hubble sphere.
>
> The speed of light isn't really about light. It's the speed limit for anything travelling through space. But space itself can stretch faster than that. The universe is expanding, not like a ball blowing up into something. It's more as if there's just more space being created all the time, everywhere. It's like raising the resolution of a photo: you take one pixel and turn it into four. That means the further away something is, the faster it's moving away from us. And at the Hubble line, things are moving away from us at the speed of light. **\[fix: 14\]** That line is roughly fourteen billion light-years away, in every direction.
>
> You'd think their light could never reach us, but it's a little more complicated than that. The Hubble sphere is also growing, so light from just beyond it can still, eventually, get here. The real edge is a little further out. That's the cosmic event horizon. Nothing that happens beyond it can ever affect us. We'll never see it, because the light will never reach us. No information, no matter, nothing. It's the limit of the unknowable.

**Are we living inside a black hole?** (rec50, the payoff of the plant)

> Now add up everything we know exists inside the Hubble sphere, and here's where it lands on our chart: right on the black hole line. **\[new →\]** The line I told you to remember. So what does that mean? Are we living inside a black hole?
>
> Well, both are a kind of horizon. A black hole's event horizon is where light that falls in can never come out. Our cosmic horizon is where nothing outside can ever come in. And both depend on the mass of what's inside. The radius of a black hole depends directly on how much stuff is in it, and the size of our Hubble sphere depends on how fast the universe expands, which depends on how much stuff is in it too. So it's no coincidence they're both on the same line.
>
> But that's where the similarities end. A black hole is an object with a centre. Our universe is just that: it's *our* universe. It's not *the* universe. If you imagine one of those galaxies that seem to be on the edge for us, well, for them, their universe is centred on them. Point a telescope the other way and they won't see empty space. They'll see a whole lot of other galaxies that are out of our reach. Because as far as we've been able to measure, the universe is practically infinite in all directions.

**The observable universe** (rewritten; no dialogue) **new**

> And here's the strange part. Ask a scientist how big the universe is, and they won't say fourteen billion light-years. They'll say forty-six billion, in every direction. That's the observable universe: not everything there is, but everything whose light has had time to reach us.
>
> Put it on the chart, and it's the one object that falls outside our triangle. That's because it isn't really one object. Its far sides have never been able to talk to each other. Forty-six billion light-years is where those galaxies are right now. But what we see is the light they sent long ago, when they were much closer, and most of them are already beyond our cosmic event horizon. Even if you could travel at the speed of light and live forever, you would never reach them. We'll never even see where they are right now.
>
> So the observable universe is less like a map of where our neighbours are, and more like a snapshot of our own history.

**Ending** (*Long only*) **new**

> So that's the top of the triangle. On the left, the black hole line: squeeze anything hard enough, and gravity wins. On the right, the Hubble sphere: stretch anything far enough, and the expansion wins. And at the very top, where the two lines meet, sits our whole universe.
>
> Which leaves one edge. If there's a limit to how big and how heavy something can be, is there a limit to how small? That's the third side of the triangle, and it's the strangest of the three.
