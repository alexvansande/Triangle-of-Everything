### Piece 2 · Black holes and dark matter (video 4, rec48, 2:14–6:15, at 1.1×)

**Fact check of part 1** (the author's 2026-11-04 text)

- **Right:** the three limits and where they sit; "from humans to planets" as what long video 1 covered;
  Jupiter giving off more heat than it gets from the Sun (about 1.7×); brown dwarfs fusing deuterium and not
  ordinary hydrogen (about 13 to 80 Jupiter masses), and deuterium too rare to last; fusion's energy pushing
  the star bigger and onto the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives; the light-pressure cap
  (the Eddington limit); the truce as one push left and one push right on the chart; the fuel running out,
  the core contracting, the next fuel igniting, the star swelling (a red giant); iron stealing energy when it
  fuses, and the core collapse that follows; the outer layers as supernova remnants or nebulae, nurseries,
  protostars, the next generation richer in heavy elements; white dwarfs for stars too small to go supernova;
  protons and electrons squeezed into neutrons in the collapse; neutron stars on the neutron's density line;
  bigger still, a black hole.
- **Wrong: "a white dwarf, a mass of degenerate iron core".** White dwarfs are made of carbon and oxygen
  (helium for the smallest, oxygen-neon-magnesium for the heaviest). Only stars above about 8 Suns get as far
  as iron, and those are the ones that go supernova, so no white dwarf is iron. Now: *"a white dwarf: that
  core of carbon and oxygen, held up by electrons that refuse to be squeezed"* (the callback to the
  super-Jupiters in long video 1 is free here; drop it if you'd rather).
- **Wrong, the same thing earlier: "until all that is left is a core made of mostly iron and other heavy
  metals"** said of every star. For the Sun and most stars the burning stops at carbon and oxygen; the iron
  core is the big stars' ending. Now: *"For most stars that's where it ends, with a core of carbon and
  oxygen. But the biggest stars keep going, element after element, until all that is left is a core of mostly
  iron."* ("Other heavy metals" dropped: the core is iron-group, iron and nickel, and "heavy metals" suggests
  gold and lead, which stars don't make this way.)
- **Kept as written, the author's call (2026-11-04): "This diagonal line is where they reach equilibrium."**
  The session's objection: the density on the line is electromagnetism's number (atoms packed together), the
  same for a hippo in orbit or a rock on the Moon, and gravity doesn't change it until Jupiter. The author's
  answer: the line is still a balance. Scale the Eiffel Tower up and its weight grows with the cube of the
  size while the strength of its material grows with the square, so past some size gravity folds it back
  toward the line (the square-cube law); from the moons up it's hydrostatic equilibrium, gravity in,
  electromagnetism out. Both are right; the sentence stays.
- **Wrong as phrased: "gravity wins over the next defense: the nucleus."** Gravity doesn't beat the nucleus;
  it helps the strong force beat the electric push between nuclei (the squeeze makes the core hot and dense
  enough for protons to get close enough to grab each other). The nucleus is never broken here; it's fused.
  Now: *"gravity wins over the next defence: the electric push that keeps nuclei apart"*, which also sets up
  your own line two paragraphs later, "gravity wins over the electric repulsion that keeps protons apart".
- **Imprecise: "they fuse into helium and other light elements."** On the main sequence hydrogen makes
  helium and nothing else (the heavier ones come later, in the giant phases). Now: *"they fuse into helium."*
- **Imprecise: "all generated in its high pressure core"** (Jupiter's heat). It comes from the whole planet
  slowly shrinking under its own weight (and some helium raining inward), not from the core as such. Now:
  *"mostly from its own weight, which is still slowly squeezing it."*
- **Fill in: "beyond X solar masses"** → *"a couple of hundred solar masses"*. The heaviest known, R136a1,
  is about 200 Suns (estimates of over 300 were revised down in 2022), and the limit is soft, more a steep
  cliff than a wall. "No known star" is the right hedge.
- **Imprecise: "will last a few billion years."** It depends on the mass: the Sun gets about ten billion,
  red dwarfs trillions, and the biggest stars only a few million. Now: *"lasts billions of years. For the
  biggest stars, only a few million."*
- **Imprecise: "the star starts to rapidly collapse"** when the hydrogen runs out. The core contracts over
  thousands to millions of years, and the outer layers swell at the same time; only the final iron collapse
  is fast (under a second). Now: *"the core starts to collapse"*, without "rapidly".
- **Imprecise: "that collapse creates even more heavier elements".** The contraction heats the core until the
  ashes of the last fuel ignite (helium, then carbon, and so on); the heavier elements come from that burning.
  Now: *"that collapse heats it enough to fuse the ashes, helium, into heavier elements."*
- **Imprecise: "Once there's no more hydrogen – or sometimes helium – to burn".** The helium runs out too,
  in every star that burns it; "sometimes" is the wrong word. Dropped, since the next sentence tells the
  sequence.
- **Fine: "a mass of pure neutrons"**: a neutron star is a few per cent protons and electrons too; now
  "almost pure".
- **Fine: "Everything is a delicate dance of a few fundamental forces"**; and "the next defence" is a good
  way to keep the scoreboard going without new terms.
- Typos fixed: "as man planet's grows", "it's outer layers", "porto-star", "enters in", "nebulas".

## 4 · Fact check of the new lines in the second half

(Part 1's fact check is with part 1, above.)

- **"These are the galaxies we weighed a minute ago: five to ten times heavier"**: as in rec48, and the
  weighing is by rotation and by the motion of galaxies in clusters, so "this is where it's doing the
  holding" is right (galaxies and clusters are bound by their dark matter).
- **"Galaxy clusters… gravity still holds them together"**: right; clusters are the largest bound things.
- **"A supercluster isn't held together… Laniakea will have come apart"**: right. Laniakea is defined by
  where galaxies are flowing, not by being bound; superclusters are not gravitationally bound, and with
  dark energy the expansion pulls them apart over tens of billions of years.
- **"A new player, and it isn't one of the four forces"**: right; dark energy is about 68 % of the
  universe's energy today and is not a force in the sense of the four. "We call it dark energy" avoids
  claiming what it is.
- **"That line is roughly fourteen billion light-years away, in every direction"**: right. The Hubble
  radius is c/H₀ ≈ 14.4 billion light-years for H₀ = 68 km/s/Mpc. The old take said thirteen.
- **"Light from just beyond it can still, eventually, get here… the cosmic event horizon… a little further
  out"**: right; the event horizon today is about 16 billion light-years out.
- **"Right on the black hole line… no coincidence"**: right; at the critical density the Hubble sphere's
  mass gives a Schwarzschild radius equal to its own radius exactly (2GM/c² = c/H).
- **"Forty-six billion, in every direction"**: right, a radius (about 46.5 billion light-years).
  **"Everything whose light has had time to reach us"**: right; that's the definition.
- **"Most of them are already beyond our cosmic event horizon"**: right. Galaxies seen out to a redshift
  of about 1.8 are still inside it; most of the observable universe's volume is beyond it. The old take's
  "everything we see is beyond" was wrong and this is the fix.
- **"At the very top, where the two lines meet, sits our whole universe"**: right on this chart: the
  Hubble sphere is where the black hole line meets the right-hand edge (the size of the universe); the
  two edges meet at the apex because of the equality above.
- Short 8's opening says "everything inside the observable universe" for brevity; the line in the script
  says the Hubble sphere, which is what lands on the black-hole line. If you want the short exact: "Add up
  everything inside the Hubble sphere…".
