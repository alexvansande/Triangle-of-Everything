## Is Jupiter a failed star? (`short-l2-failed-star`, 1:29)

> We tend to think of planets and stars as completely different things, but that's not really true. Take, for example, Jupiter. Almost half of the energy that keeps its temperature inside it, it's not coming from the sun, it's actually coming from inside its own core. As it squeezes together it generates heat. Here the delicate tension now favours gravity.

> There's so much weight that the atoms themselves are crushed. The electrons are stripped of their nuclei, leaving what we call degenerate matter, a bare nuclei in a sea of electrons. That sea of electrons is what keeping the planet from being squeezed any further. So as a planet grows to be more massive than Jupiter, it doesn't actually get bigger, it just gets denser.

> Increase the mass any further and we reach brown dwarfs, a weird in-between object, too big to be a planet, too small to be a star. Now the gravity is making such a high pressure, not only it's winning over that electron pressure, but it's fusing hydrogen together. But not common hydrogen. It can only fuse a very rare kind of hydrogen called deuterium that comes with an extra neutron.

> Deuterium is so rare that brown dwarfs just can't keep on shining for very long. They just sit there cooling slowly, glowing like an ember for billions of years.


## What makes a star a star? (`short-l2-what-makes-a-star`, 1:18)

> Add a little bit more mass and something magical happens because a new force comes into play. The strong force, as the name implies, is the strongest of all forces, but it also has a very limited range. In here now gravity is squeezing the protons together so much that the strong force is able to grab them together and create a new kind of nuclei.

> It's not hydrogen anymore, it's helium. It turns two hydrogens into one helium and releases a lot of energy. And that energy will now create an outward pressure, pushing our objects again to the right. The more massive a star is, the brighter it shines and it creates this diagonal line, which is called main sequence and is where stars will live most of their lives.

> But that line also has a limit, because if a star is too bright, then its outer layers of their atmospheres are being thrown so violently outside that it escapes its own gravity well, and it just expands as a cloud. We are not sure exactly where it ends, but around here are the largest stars we know.


## How do stars die? (`short-l2-how-stars-die`, 1:33)

> But that equilibrium is kept by the fusion of hydrogen, and once it runs out, that equilibrium goes haywire. With no more hydrogen to burn, the core shrinks under its own weight, and that squeeze heats it up until the next fuel catches, helium. That pushes a star out again, far bigger than before, a red giant now burning helium.

> For a star like the sun, that's the end of the road. It puffs its outer layers away and leaves its core behind. But the biggest stars will go on, shrink, ignite, swell again and again, burning carbon, then oxygen, then silicon, until the core is iron. And iron is where magic dies, because fusing it costs energy instead of giving it away, so the fire suddenly goes out and the core collapses in less than a single second.

> Either way, the star is now split in two, two distinct objects on our chart. The outer layers will keep expanding with the leftover energy from the last battle and become supernova remnants or nebulae. These will eventually meet others and merge into a larger cloud of gas and dust called the stellar nursery which will eventually collapse back into a protostar and start a new cycle.

> But this time the next generation will start with some richer materials that could lead to a planet full of life, like ours.


## What's left when a star dies? (`short-l2-whats-left`, 1:08)

> The core, however, will stay put. If the star isn't massive enough to go supernova, it will stay as a white dwarf, a core of carbon and oxygen. But if it does go supernova, that catastrophic core collapse will be so powerful that a new player force will enter in play. That's the weak force. Protons and electrons will be squeezed into a mass of mostly neutrons, a neutron star.

> A neutrino will be sent out carrying a huge amount of energy. And because neutrons, unlike protons and electrons, do not repel each other, it will just stay put. A neutron star is really just a very big atomic nuclei, mostly a bunch of neutrons held together not by the strong force, but by pure gravity.

> And if the core is any bigger, then something even weirder will happen. It will collapse into a black hole. An object so weird that it deserves a video of its own.


## The dance of the forces (`short-l2-dance-of-the-forces`, 3:19; recorded on its own, rec58, 2026-11-08)

> Everything in our universe is a delicate dance between four fundamental forces and, well, sometimes it can be a violent tug of war also.
>
> If you plot everything that exists in a chart of mass and size, you see that most things form this diagonal line. This is where the equilibrium of that particular tug of war between electromagnetism and gravity. Make something too big and it will crush under its own weight. Try to squeeze it too hard and the electrons themselves will push it outwards. The resulting line, this diagonal, is actually the density of atoms, and everything made of ordinary matter will fall somewhere along here.
>
> But you can see that giant planets like Jupiter and beyond do not fall on this diagonal line, rather they just go up a column. That's because their core is not made of ordinary matter, but degenerate matter. This happens because they are so massive and there's so much pressure in their core that it is able to win over the repulsion of the electrons. So they break out of their clouds and what we have is a bunch of nuclei and a sea of electrons. That creates a new equilibrium as quantum effects prevent them from being crushed any further.
>
> But if you add even more mass then something magical happens because a new force comes into play. The strong force, as the name implies, is the strongest of all forces, but it also has a very limited range. For some very massive objects their gravity will be so strong they will push those cores together that the strong force can now capture them and make one single nucleus.
>
> So that's fusion. First on brown dwarfs with a very small percentage of their hydrogen, then on stars that can burn all of their hydrogen. That fusion creates a massive amount of energy and that now pushes the objects even further.
>
> And now that delicate dance between gravity and electromagnetism and the strong force will create this new stable equilibrium diagonal here, which is called the main sequence, where stars will live most of their lives. But that equilibrium is only kept by the fusion of hydrogen, and once that runs out, that goes haywire.
>
> And when that happens, a catastrophic core collapse will be so powerful that a new player force will enter into play. That's the weak force and that's what decides what the nucleus even is. Protons and electrons squeezed together will create a mass of neutrons, and that creates a neutron star.
>
> And when that happens, a sea of neutrinos is sent out carrying a huge amount of energy. And because neutrons, unlike protons and electrons, do not repel each other, they will just stay put.
>
> So a neutron star is really just a very big atomic nucleus, mostly a bunch of neutrons held together not by the strong force but purely by the force of gravity
