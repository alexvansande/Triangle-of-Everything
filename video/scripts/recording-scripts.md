## 2 · Up the line (`short-02-up-the-line`)

### As cut now
(rec32, 2026-10-23)

> If for some reason you wanted to draw everything that exists into the same chart, probably a good way is to use a log of mass and size. If you do that you notice that almost all things will fall along those diagonal lines. And it's not hard to see why. In the metric system, one gram of water is exactly one milliliter and that will fill a cube exactly one centimeter wide. But since most things aren't really cubes, we'll be measuring size as a sphere. Since this is a log, in most cases it means just moving things a little bit to the side. But that's okay. Now imagine that you multiply all three sides by ten, ten centimeters. In a log chart that means walking one unit to the side, you've just increased the volume by a thousand, to one liter. Since liquid water has a consistent density of one, that means one kilogram, so you walked three units up in the chart. If you do that again, multiply the size by 10, and you'll have 1000 times the weight, that's one ton. All of this creates this diagonal line. That is the density of water. Everything to the left of this will by definition be denser than water, and it sinks. Here we have objects made of stone, of metal. Anything to the right will float. Similarly, you could draw a diagonal line for the density of air at one atmosphere in Earth. This will separate things lighter or heavier than air. And as the name indicates, you can see lighter than air aircraft. Those are balloons and zeppelins.
>
> Most animals, however, live in a much narrower band. That's because we are mostly made of water. Some of us are heavier than others, for example, though the hippo can't actually swim, they just bounce along the riverbed.
>
> As we go up, we see heavier and heavier animals, like the elephants or dinosaurs, the largest animals that ever walked the Earth. However, the largest animal ever is still with us, that's the blue whale. And a little bit above that, we have the largest tree, that's the sequoia. Now we are done with life and we are in engineering projects. You can see for example that the Eiffel Tower is actually surprisingly light. That's because we are also counting all the internal space. The Eiffel Tower is in many ways fractal.
>
> And then we move to lots of big rocks and there's some intersection between them, like the Great Pyramid which is basically just a giant rock. Higher than that, now we have lakes and asteroids, and then we arrive at small moons. And something very interesting happens when we talk about those moons. As we go up, the moons start becoming less and less potato shaped and more spherical. This is the moment where gravity becomes stronger than the molecular bonds that keep things in their shape. And after this, everything might as well behave as a liquid, so they all become spherical. From here on, gravity dominates everything. Another example is that the Moon and Mercury have no atmosphere, while Mars has almost none. Venus and Earth do have a very dense atmosphere, but the lighter gases like hydrogen and helium would just float away. And because most of the universe is actually those two gases, once you have enough gravity to hold on to them, you'll become mostly made of them. That's why we see the gas giant planets. And you see that reflected on the graph. Most of those dots stop being on the left, with the rocky planets, and move to the right, where they are mostly made of gas. Saturn is actually less dense than water. Objects keep climbing, and then suddenly the trend stops. You see that you can't get a planet much bigger than Jupiter. Even objects ten times, a hundred times more massive than Jupiter are still roughly Jupiter sized. This is because the pressure in the core is now so large that electrons get squeezed out of their atoms and it becomes degenerate matter. And then, just around brown dwarfs, we have a huge bifurcation, in which some objects veer to the right and some veer to the left, because now we are not talking about the history of ordinary matter anymore. We are talking about the life and death of stars.

## 3 · Planets into stars (`short-03-stars`)

### As cut now
(rec36, 2026-10-27, plays at 1.1×)

> We tend to think of stars and planets as completely different things, but that's not really true. In our own solar system, Jupiter and Saturn give off more heat than they get from the Sun, mostly because their own mass keeps squeezing their cores. And as mass grows, so does the pressure in the core.
>
> Until some point, they become brown dwarfs. Brown dwarfs are really in-betweens, not quite stars, not quite planets. They're just massive enough to fuse a rare kind of hydrogen called deuterium, but not ordinary hydrogen. Deuterium is so rare that brown dwarfs just can't keep shining on like stars.
>
> But add a little bit more mass and you get the split you see on this chart. Once the pressure in the core is high enough to fuse ordinary hydrogen, that fusion releases some energy that creates a force that will push the star outwards, making it bigger, and it will move into this other diagonal line that we call the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives.
>
> Until the hydrogen runs out, and then as the fuel for fusion is used up, the star collapses and then expands again and collapses and expands and splits in two. The outer layers are blown away as supernova or planetary nebula, and the inner core becomes a very dense object.
>
> As those supernova and nebulae spread out, they meet others, and together they make what we call stellar nurseries. Huge clouds of gas that pull together, collapse into protostars, and then into stars again, and the cycle begins.
>
> And what about the dense core I told you about? Something really interesting happens here. If a star isn't massive enough to go supernova, then once fuel runs out, what's left is a white dwarf. But if it's big enough to go supernova, the pressure in its core is so huge that protons and electrons get squeezed together into neutrons, and it becomes a neutron star, so dense that a single spoonful would weigh as much as a mountain. And if the star is any bigger still, the core collapses into a black hole, an object so strange that it deserves a video of its own.

## 4 · Black holes and dark matter (`short-04-black-holes`)
