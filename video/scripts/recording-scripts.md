## 3 · Planets into stars (`short-03-stars`)

### As cut now
(rec36, 2026-10-27, plays at 1.1×)

> We tend to think of stars and planets as completely different things, but that's not really true. In our own solar system, Jupiter and Saturn give off more heat than they get from the Sun, mostly because their own mass keeps squeezing their cores. And as mass grows, so does the pressure in the core.
>
> Until some point, they become brown dwarfs. Brown dwarfs are really in-betweens, not quite stars, not quite planets. They're just massive enough to fuse a rare kind of hydrogen called deuterium, but not ordinary hydrogen. Deuterium is so rare that brown dwarfs just can't keep shining on like stars.
>
> But add a little bit more mass and you get the split you see on this chart. Once the pressure in the core is high enough to fuse ordinary hydrogen, that fusion releases some energy that creates a force that will push the star outwards, making it bigger, and it will move into this other diagonal line that we call the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives.
>
> Until the hydrogen runs out, and then as the fuel for fusion is used up, the star collapses and then expands again and collapses and expands and splits in two. The outer layers are blown away as supernova or planetary nebula, and the inner core becomes a very dense object.
>
> As those supernova and nebulae spread out, they meet others, and together they make what we call stellar nurseries. Huge clouds of gas that pull together, collapse into protostars, and then into stars again, and the cycle begins.
>
> And what about the dense core I told you about? Something really interesting happens here. If a star isn't massive enough to go supernova, then once fuel runs out, what's left is a white dwarf. But if it's big enough to go supernova, the pressure in its core is so huge that protons and electrons get squeezed together into neutrons, and it becomes a neutron star, so dense that a single spoonful would weigh as much as a mountain. And if the star is any bigger still, the core collapses into a black hole, an object so strange that it deserves a video of its own.

And it's a very important line, because it's the top left edge of our triangle. Any object that becomes too small for its mass will collapse into a black hole, and every black hole lives exactly on this line, meaning that there's no object that could ever exist that lives above or to the left of it. A black hole doesn't really have a size. It's thought to be a singularity, a point, but it does have an event horizon, the distance from which nothing can escape, and that grows in step with its mass. That's why the edge is a straight 45-degree line. Squeeze the Sun into a black hole and it would be about six kilometers wide, and the Earth would be the size of a marble. > That's why the Compton line runs at a 45-degree angle. At this scale we look at energy instead of mass (thanks to E = mc², they're equivalent), and at wavelength instead of size. The more energy, the shorter the wavelength. And that's one of the limits of our triangle.
>
> Now let's go back to visible light. This is Earth as you would see it from space, and the sky behind it, in the light our eyes can see. But colour isn't something out there in the world. If our eyes were tuned just a little differently, we would see infrared as red, red as green, and green as blue. Look: the forests light up, because plants reflect a lot of infrared. Or we could shift the other way, like a bee does, and see ultraviolet as blue. So is the red we see the same as the red everyone sees? Not if you're a bee: bees can't see red at all.
>

Further up, in X-rays, Earth goes dark: our atmosphere stops X-rays completely, which is lucky for us. But the sky lights up with hot gas at millions of degrees, and pairs of stars where one is eating the other. In gamma rays, the most energetic light there is, we see the plane of our galaxy glowing where cosmic rays smash into gas, spinning dead stars, and the cores of distant galaxies with giant black holes at their centre.
>
> Now let's go all the way back down to infrared, where we see heat. Warm land glows; cold cloud tops are dark. In the sky, infrared goes straight through the dust that hides the centre of our galaxy, and we see the stars behind it. Further down, we see the dust itself: cold clouds where new stars are born. And in microwaves, Earth shows its water: vapour, rain, sea ice. And the sky is filled with the oldest light in the universe: the afterglow of the Big Bang.
>
> And that's the interesting part, because not all light comes from the same place. So let's put everything on a log scale, like we do on the chart. It's the only way to see the whole universe. If Earth is this big, that's the Moon's orbit, just over one light-second away. Then the Sun, eight light-minutes away. And then the closest stars, a few light-years away. Zoom out further and the sky becomes a cloud of dots: the stars around us, then our galaxy, and then the entire observable universe.
>
> Now let's go through the spectrum again. Visible light comes mostly from stars in our own neighbourhood of the galaxy. Infrared reaches further, through the dust. Radio shows our galaxy's magnetic field, and galaxies billions of light-years away. Microwaves come from the very edge: the oldest light there is. And X-rays and gamma rays come from the most violent things in the universe: exploding stars and feeding black holes. Every wavelength is a different window, and each one shows a different universe.
