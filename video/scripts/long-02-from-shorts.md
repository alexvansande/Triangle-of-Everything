## 1 · Stars: The Life and Death of Stars (`short-03-stars`, 2:27)

We tend to think of stars and planets as completely different things, but that's not really true. In our own solar system, Jupiter and Saturn give off more heat than they get from the Sun, mostly because their own mass keeps squeezing their cores. And as mass grows, so does the pressure in the core. Until some point they become brown dwarfs. Brown dwarfs are really in-betweens, not quite stars, not quite planets, they're just massive enough to fuse a rare kind of hydrogen called deuterium, but not ordinary hydrogen. Deuterium is so rare that brown dwarfs just can't keep shining on like stars. But add a little bit more mass and you get the split you see on this chart. Once the pressure in the core is high enough to fuse ordinary hydrogen, that fusion releases some energy that creates a force that will push the star outwards, making it bigger, and it will move into this other diagonal line that we call the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives. Until the hydrogen runs out, and then as the fuel for fusion is used up, the star collapses and then expands again and collapses and expands and splits in two. The outer layers are blown away as supernova or planetary nebula, and the inner core becomes a very dense object. As those supernova and nebulae spread out, they meet others, and together they make what we call stellar nurseries. Huge clouds of gas that pull together, collapse into protostars, and then into stars again, and the cycle begins. And what about the dense core I told you about? Something really interesting happens here. If a star isn't massive enough to go supernova, then once fuel runs out, what's left is a white dwarf. But if it's big enough to go supernova, the pressure in its core is so huge that protons and electrons get squeezed together into neutrons, and it becomes a neutron star, so dense that a single spoonful would weigh as much as a mountain. And if the star is any bigger still, the core collapses into a black hole, ~~an object so strange that it deserves a video of its own.~~ *[the Short's cliffhanger; in the long video the black holes come next: *"…collapses into a black hole. And black holes are strange enough to be worth a closer look."*]*

## 2 · The black-hole line: The Black Hole Slope (`short-04a-black-hole-line`, 2:02)

~~I've been doing a series of videos explaining this chart, which I call the triangle of everything, because it contains literally everything on a log of mass and size. In the last video we saw that this chart could show how stars die and leave a dense core behind, which can be a white dwarf, a neutron star, or the one I want to talk about today, a black hole.~~ *[intro and recap of the stars' ending, 20 s; the chapter can open straight on "Most objects in this chart are a bunch of little dots, but black holes are not."]* Most objects in this chart are a bunch of little dots, but black holes are not. They all sit exactly on one line, called the Schwarzschild radius. And it's a very important line, because it's the top left edge of our triangle. Any object that becomes too small for its mass will collapse into a black hole, and every black hole lives exactly on this line, meaning that there's no object that could ever exist that lives above or to the left of it. A black hole doesn't really have a size. It's thought to be a singularity, a point, but it does have an event horizon, the distance from which nothing can escape, and that grows in step with its mass. That's why the edge is a straight 45-degree line. Squeeze the Sun into a black hole and it would be about six kilometers wide, and the Earth would be the size of a marble. The black holes we know formed in the collapsing core of dying stars, or just grew by swallowing gas and merging with other black holes. They can go from a few Suns to billions of stars. The one in the centre of the Milky Way, for example, has 4 million solar masses. And here's a strange part: the bigger they get, the less dense they are. The really supermassive ones are less dense than water: they would float if you had a pool big enough. Like M87, the first black hole ever to be photographed, or TON 618, one of the largest we know of. Its event horizon is bigger than Pluto's orbit. But the most interesting question isn't actually how big a black hole can be, but how small? Because there are actually some people who believe that most of the things in the universe are just tiny black holes.
