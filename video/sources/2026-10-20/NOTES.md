## Fact check (rec21)

Right: the chart is a density scatter plot with size across and mass up; stars form
in nurseries, become protostars and live on the main sequence, then swell into red
giants; white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes are the dense cores left
behind; all known black holes sit on the Schwarzschild line, the top-left limit
that nothing crosses; anything compact enough becomes a black hole; stellar black
holes are about 5 to 100 Suns (the lightest known are about 3–5); Sgr A* is about
4 million Suns; the biggest are less dense than water; M87* was the first
photographed (2019); TON 618, about 40–66 billion Suns (the chart uses 66), has a
horizon larger than the planets' orbits; in theory any mass can be squeezed into a
black hole; the early universe may have made them; quasar light (the Lyman-alpha
forest), the CMB, gravitational waves and microlensing each rule out a range of
masses, and Hawking radiation the lightest; what's left is about a small moon's
mass in a virus-sized hole down to an asteroid's in something smaller than an atom;
they'd be dark and felt only through gravity; galaxies and clusters weigh several to
ten times their visible matter; the window may close further with neutron-star
studies; galaxies sit on their own diagonal, mostly empty space; the largest
structures reach the average density of the universe, and voids are emptier still;
the line leads to the Hubble radius.

Wrong, left out of the cut:
1. "and then explode in a supernova" as what stars do after the red giant: only
   stars above about 8 Suns do; Sun-like ones shed a planetary nebula. The cut goes
   from "become red giants" to "depending on their mass, stars will always leave a
   dense core behind".
2. "black holes are typically the size of a star": a few Suns' mass, yes, but the
   size is the horizon, about 10–300 km, far smaller than any star. The cut starts
   at "And as they grow massive and bigger they actually become less dense".
3. "nebulae and galaxies and clusters… much heavier than they ought to be": nebulae
   (gas clouds) hold almost no dark matter. Cut to "galaxies and clusters of
   galaxies". The other take, "galaxies and clusters and nebulae, we find them to be
   sometimes 10 times more heavy", is only used from "sometimes".

Wrong, still in the cut (worth a re-take if convenient):
4. "**All** of the black holes we know of are formed in the collapsing core of a
   dying star" (and "the ones we know have been formed in the collapsing core of
   dying stars"): true of the stellar ones; the supermassive ones (Sgr A*, M87*, TON
   618, named right after) grew by swallowing gas and merging, from seeds we don't
   know yet. Suggested: "The black holes we know of start in the collapsing core of a
   dying star, or grow, by swallowing gas and each other, to millions or billions of
   Suns."

Imprecise, optional:
5. "an object with the mass of **a moon**": a small moon (about 10²² g, a 200 km
   one; our Moon is 7 × 10²⁵ g, far above the window). The scene labels Mimas, "a
   small moon". "compressed to the size of an atom": a few hundred times smaller
   than an atom (scene: "smaller than an atom").
6. "each of those surveys limit the **sizes**": masses (for black holes it's the
   same thing, as size follows mass).
7. "the density of **empty space**", "as dense as the vacuum of space": galaxies are
   mostly empty space, about 10⁻²⁴ g/cm³ on average; the largest structures reach
   the universe's average, about 10⁻²⁹ g/cm³; voids are a few times emptier. "Empty
   space" isn't zero: the scene says "mostly empty space".
8. "wouldn't require a new particle or new physics": no new particle, but making
   enough of them needs something unusual in the early universe (as in rec18–20).
9. "hundreds and thousands … of suns": intermediate-mass black holes are rare and
   debated; fine as a range.

## Compared with the first video 5 (rec18–20): what the new take leaves out

- **Why the line is 45°** (significant): rec18 explained that a black hole has no
  real size, only an event horizon, whose diameter is proportional to its mass, so
  the Schwarzschild line has a 1 : 1 slope. The new take names the Schwarzschild
  radius but not why it's a straight 45° line. Suggested insert, after "…and never
  cross it": "A black hole doesn't really have a size: what we draw is its event
  horizon, and its width grows in step with its mass. That's why this line is a
  perfectly straight 45 degrees."
- Andromeda's black hole (minor; the Milky Way's, now with its 4 million Suns, stays).
- The "aliens with a machine that builds a black hole" aside (minor).
- "Most of the things we've seen so far are on this atomic diagonal line" is still
  there in a new form ("unlike most of the objects … following the density line of
  atoms").

New in this take: Sgr A*'s 4 million Suns, stellar black holes at 5–100 Suns, TON
618's 60 billion Suns, each survey named (quasars, CMB, gravitational waves,
microlensing), the window as moon-to-virus and asteroid-to-atom, ten times more
mass than expected, the neutron-star studies that may close the window, and the
empty-space framing of the galaxies' diagonal with the voids.
