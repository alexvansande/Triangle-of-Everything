### Fact check

Right:
- Things made of ordinary matter sit on one diagonal until brown dwarfs, then
  split: degenerate matter to the left, stars and giants to the right (video 4).
- Galaxy clusters are less dense than galaxies because of the space between them.
- Voids aren't empty, just emptier than average; on the chart they sit with
  Laniakea, near the average density of the universe.
- The Schwarzschild line: nothing sits beyond it, black holes sit on it.
- The farther away, the faster things recede (Hubble's law), and past a certain
  distance faster than light. That's allowed: it's space expanding, not something
  moving through space.
- Beyond our horizon things probably look the same as here: an assumption (the
  cosmological principle), not something we can check.
- Every observer has their own observable universe, centred on them.
- Is the universe a black hole? No, and "a black hole has a centre, our horizon is
  centred on whoever is looking" is a good reason to give.
- All the horizons fall on one line, the critical density, about the density of the
  biggest empty structures. Yes.

Wrong, with a fix:
- **"Everything larger than a star is on this other diagonal density line."** Not
  one line: the bigger the structure, the emptier. On the chart: nebulae and
  globular clusters about 10⁻²² g/cm³, galaxies about 10⁻²⁴ (one atom per cm³, the
  galaxies' line from video 5), clusters 10⁻²⁷·⁷, Laniakea and the voids about
  10⁻³⁰. It levels off at the average density of the universe, about 10⁻²⁹ g/cm³,
  five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. Say: "the bigger the structure, the emptier
  it gets, until at the largest scales everything is just the average density of
  the universe".
- **"The density of the vacuum of space."** The galaxies' line is one atom per
  cm³, a million times denser than the average. "Mostly empty space" is fine;
  "the density of the vacuum" isn't, since dark energy is that.
- **"Nothing that happens here [at the Hubble radius] can ever be seen by us or
  affect us."** That's the cosmic event horizon, about 16 billion light-years away,
  not the Hubble radius (14.4 billion). The Hubble radius is only where things
  recede at the speed of light *right now*. We see plenty from beyond it: light
  sent long ago, from up to 46 billion light-years (the observable universe), and
  light sent today from between 14 and 16 billion light-years will still reach us.
  Say: "This edge is the Hubble radius: the distance where space carries things
  away from us at the speed of light. Nothing bigger than that can act as one
  thing, so nothing on the chart is bigger."
- **"The universe is actually infinite, as far as we've calculated."** We've
  measured it flat to within a fraction of a percent, which fits an infinite
  universe, but a finite one much bigger than what we see fits too. Say "it may
  well be infinite".
- **"The Hubble sphere is an event horizon, like a black hole's."** It isn't
  one. The cosmic event horizon is, and it's close by (16 against 14 billion
  light-years), so the comparison still works if you name it, and it's inside out:
  from a black hole nothing gets out, from beyond our horizon nothing gets in.
- **"We can see things outside it… if we plot that it would be on this other
  line."** It's the same line. All three horizons sit on the critical-density line,
  further up the more mass they hold. Only the Hubble sphere lands on the
  black-hole line; the observable universe (46 billion light-years) would sit well
  past it, in the "forbidden" corner. That's fine: the Schwarzschild limit is for a
  lump of mass in empty space, and the universe isn't one.
- **Laniakea: "all our galaxies are in those weird lines called the Laniakea
  superclusters".** Laniakea is one supercluster, ours: about 100,000 galaxies over
  500 million light-years, strung along filaments (the "weird lines" are the
  cosmic web). Say "our galaxy is one of about a hundred thousand in Laniakea".

Imprecise, fine to keep:
- The chart's "Observable Universe" dot is really the Hubble sphere (10²⁸·¹⁴ cm =
  14.6 billion ly, 9×10⁵⁵ g). Worth either saying "the Hubble sphere" in the
  narration, or renaming the dot (and its article) before this video.
- "Largest things made of matter": superclusters are the largest we map as single
  structures. Bigger patterns (walls, giant arcs) are claimed, and disputed.
- It's video 6, not 5.

### The horizons, for the narration (H₀ = 67.7 km/s/Mpc)

| Horizon | Radius today | What it means | On the chart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hubble sphere | 14.4 billion ly (1.37×10²⁸ cm) | space recedes at exactly c | exactly on the black-hole line, 9×10⁵⁵ g |
| Cosmic event horizon | ~16 billion ly | events happening now beyond it will never reach us | just past the line |
| Particle horizon (observable universe) | 46 billion ly | the farthest light that has reached us so far | well past it, ~3×10⁵⁷ g |

All three at the critical density, 8.6×10⁻³⁰ g/cm³ (≈ 5 hydrogen atoms per m³: 5%
ordinary matter, 26% dark matter, 69% dark energy).

Why the Hubble sphere is on the black-hole line, in one sentence: the critical
density is *defined* as the density at which a sphere the size of the Hubble radius
holds exactly the mass whose Schwarzschild radius is the Hubble radius
(2GM/c² = c/H). Not a coincidence, and it was true at every moment: as the universe
aged, its Hubble sphere slid up the black-hole line, crossing the epoch lines
already in the chart (Planck, GUT, electroweak, nuclear, first nuclei, CMB, now).

### Fact check (rec26 as cut)

Right: everything made of atoms on one diagonal, splitting at brown dwarfs; bigger
means emptier from nebulae to voids; a galaxy averages about one atom per cm³; groups,
clusters, Laniakea (about 100,000 galaxies, ~500 million light-years); voids are

same average-density line and outside the triangle.

Imprecise, worth knowing:
1. **"it's sort of an illusion, because the light that reaches us is not there"**: the
   observable universe isn't an illusion. The galaxies whose light we now receive
   really are about 46 billion ly away today; what we see is how they looked when they
   were much closer. What makes it fall outside the triangle is that it isn't one
   connected thing: no signal could cross it, so the triangle's "one object" rule
   doesn't apply. A re-record could say: "That point sits outside the triangle, because
   it isn't one object: its far sides have never been able to reach each other. We see
   them only as they were, long ago, when they were much closer."
2. "Nothing can travel through space faster than light, but space itself can": space
   doesn't move; distances grow. Fine as a popular phrasing.
3. "Anything that happens here, right now" (said "here", meaning there).
4. The chart's "Observable Universe" dot sits at the Hubble radius (10²⁸·¹⁴ cm), so the
   video labels it "Hubble sphere", while the map's own label still says "Observable
   Universe". Renaming the object (`src/objects.json`) would fix it on the site too.
