### Fact check
- **Right:** everything a dance between four fundamental forces, sometimes a violent tug of war; most things on one
  diagonal in a chart of mass and size; too big and it crushes under its own weight, squeezed too hard and the electrons
  push back; the diagonal as the density of atoms and of everything made of ordinary matter; the giant planets going up a
  column from Jupiter; a core of degenerate matter; the atoms broken open into nuclei in a sea of electrons; a new
  equilibrium from quantum effects (degeneracy pressure); the strong force the strongest and the shortest in reach; gravity
  pushing nuclei together until the strong force can grab them; fusion first in brown dwarfs then in stars; the energy
  pushing the star out onto a new equilibrium, the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives; the equilibrium
  kept by hydrogen fusion; the weak force stepping in at the collapse; protons and electrons into neutrons, a neutron star;
  a sea of neutrinos carrying the energy (about 99 %); a neutron star as one giant nucleus held together by gravity.
- **A fragment, kept: "This is where the equilibrium of that particular tug of war between electromagnetism and
  gravity."** No verb; spoken it reads as a line that trails into "Make something too big…". If re-recorded: *"This is where
  that particular tug of war, between electromagnetism and gravity, reaches its equilibrium."* (And, as settled on
  2026-11-05: for small things gravity isn't in the balance; the author's tug-of-war framing stands.)
- **Imprecise: "so much pressure in their core that it is able to win over the repulsion of the electrons"** (as in rec54 and
  rec57): what the squeeze beats is the electrons' binding to their nuclei; the author's own wording, kept.
- **Imprecise: "brown dwarfs, with a very small percentage of their hydrogen"**: deuterium, 1 in 40,000 (0.003 %): "a very
  small percentage" is generous but the idea is right. **"then on stars that can burn all of their hydrogen"**: a star burns
  only its core's hydrogen, about a tenth of it. *"then on stars, which can burn ordinary hydrogen."*
- **Imprecise: "once that runs out… a catastrophic core collapse"**: only stars over about eight Suns collapse; the Short
  skips the Sun's gentler end (the white dwarf), which is fine for a Short about the forces, but the sentence reads as if
  every star collapses. *"and for the biggest stars, a catastrophic core collapse…"*
- **Imprecise: "because neutrons do not repel each other, they will just stay put"** (as in rec57): what stops the collapse
  is the neutrons' refusal to be squeezed and the strong force's hard core, not the absence of repulsion. *"And neutrons,
  packed shoulder to shoulder, refuse to be squeezed any further, so it just stays put."*
- **"the strong force can now capture them and make one single nucleus"** then **"two protons"**: the animation shows what
  the words skip (four hydrogens, two turned into neutrons by the weak force); fine.
- **The four hydrogens / deuterium line** (the author's question of 2026-11-08, answered in the 2026-11-06 notes) isn't in
  this take; *"deuterium comes with its neutron already, so it skips the hard step"* remains a line to add.
